## Getting There Is Part of It

Patagonia doesn't let you arrive easily. The nearest city with reliable international flights is Punta Arenas, a three-hour bus ride from Puerto Natales, which is itself a two-hour drive from Torres del Paine National Park. The journey is part of the experience — you arrive having already traveled through a landscape that feels progressively more remote, more windswept, more like the edge of something.

## The W Trek

The W Trek takes its name from the rough shape it traces through the park's highlights. Done over four to five days, it covers about 80 kilometers and passes through three of the park's main zones:

- **Valle del Francés** — A hanging valley flanked by glaciers and towering granite walls. The wind here is extraordinary — not unpleasant, but physical, something you walk against.
- **Los Cuernos** — Distinctive peaks of dark sedimentary rock capping lighter granite, the result of geological collision that looks almost deliberate.
- **Base Las Torres** — The final destination for most trekkers. The three famous granite towers rising above a glacial lake. You hike two hours in the dark to reach this viewpoint at sunrise, and it is worth every step.

## What to Know Before You Go

Patagonian weather is notoriously fast-changing. I experienced sunshine, horizontal rain, and hail within the same afternoon on day three. Pack layers, a genuinely waterproof shell, and assume conditions will be worse than the forecast.

The refugios along the route are comfortable and the food is better than you'd expect. Booking well in advance — months ahead for the busy season — is not optional.

## The Silence

What I remember most isn't the towers or the glaciers. It's the silence of the early mornings, when the wind temporarily stills and the mountains reflect in the lakes, and you remember that most of the world is not like this.
