## It Wasn't Invented to Survive a Nuclear War

That's the myth, anyway. ARPANET — the US Defense Department research network that became the internet's ancestor — was funded by the military, but its actual designers were university researchers trying to solve a much more mundane problem: how to share expensive computing resources across institutions without driving to each other's campuses.

The first ARPANET message was sent on October 29, 1969, between UCLA and Stanford Research Institute. The intended message was "login." The system crashed after "lo." An accidental first word for a network that would eventually carry everything.

## The Protocols That Made It Work

ARPANET connected a handful of nodes. The internet — a *network of networks* — required something more fundamental: a shared language. That language arrived in 1983 with TCP/IP, designed by Vint Cerf and Bob Kahn. Any network that spoke TCP/IP could join the internet, regardless of its underlying hardware. This principle of open interconnection is why the internet grew as fast as it did.

Key milestones after that:

- **1991** — Tim Berners-Lee publishes the World Wide Web, layering hypertext documents on top of the internet's infrastructure.
- **1993** — The Mosaic browser brings the web to non-technical users with images and clickable links.
- **1995–2000** — The dot-com boom (and bust) pours capital into commercial internet infrastructure.
- **2004–2010** — Social networks, smartphones, and broadband reshape daily life more profoundly than any previous media technology.

## What Didn't Change

Through every phase, the internet's core architecture has remained stubbornly decentralized. Packets still route around damage or congestion. Anyone can still publish a server and reach the world. That design decision, made by a handful of researchers in the 1970s, remains the most consequential architectural choice in computing history.
